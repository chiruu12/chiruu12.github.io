Judging at Zenith School of AI

October 2026

On 31 October I am going to Zenith School of AI in Gurgaon to judge a student hackathon.

The format is an overnight build against a real industry problem statement, somewhere between twelve and fifteen hours, with submissions evaluated the next morning. The part that made me say yes is what happens after. The students are not building toward a prize and then stopping. The hackathon is the on-ramp to contributing to open source, and the projects are meant to turn into upstream work.

That changes what is worth rewarding.

// what I will be looking for

Whether the thing runs on a machine that is not theirs. A README that assumes your exact Python version and three environment variables you were never told about is the single most common reason a good project goes nowhere, and it is the same reason a first pull request gets closed without review. Clone it fresh, follow your own instructions, see what breaks.

Whether they can say what does not work yet. A submission that names its own failure cases is more convincing than one that claims everything is handled, because the second one is never true and every reviewer knows it. Being specific about the gap is a sign you understand the problem, not a sign you ran out of time.

Scope. Twelve hours is enough to do one thing properly and not enough to do four things at all. The teams that pick one narrow piece of the problem statement and finish it will read better than the ones with five half-wired features and a slide about the roadmap.

Whether the commit history looks like people working. Not as a process check, just as a signal that the work was built rather than pasted in at hour eleven.

// the thing I actually want to tell them

The gap between a hackathon project and a merged upstream contribution is smaller than it looks, and it is almost entirely about the parts that are not code. Reading the contributing guide. Getting someone else's test suite to run. Writing a description a stranger can evaluate in ten seconds.

My first contributions were not clever. They were small and correct and explained properly, in projects where somebody was clearly overloaded and the issue tracker showed it. That path is open to every student in that room on the 31st, and most of them do not know it yet.

I will write up how it went afterwards.
